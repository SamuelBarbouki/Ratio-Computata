Steven Byrnes has written a valuable essay. In [“6 reasons why ‘alignment-is-hard’ discourse seems alien to human intuitions, and vice-versa,”](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/d4HNRdw6z7Xqbnu5E/6-reasons-why-alignment-is-hard-discourse-seems-alien-to?utm_campaign=post_share&utm_source=link) he identifies something real: a mechanism in human cognition that orients us toward prosocial behavior without external constraint. He calls it “Approval Reward”—an innate drive to seek the approval of others, operating even when no one is watching, generating what he calls “proud, self-reflective, ego-syntonic” desires rather than mere calculated compliance.

The observation is correct. The mechanism is real. The implications for alignment are significant.

But the essay suffers from a peculiar blindness. Byrnes writes as if he were discovering something new. He is not. What he calls “Approval Reward” has been analyzed with far greater precision, over six decades, under a different name: _mimetic desire_.

The bibliography he does not cite includes: René Girard’s _Mensonge romantique et vérité romanesque_ (1961), _La Violence et le Sacré_ (1972), _Des choses cachées depuis la fondation du monde_ (1978); Jean-Pierre Dupuy’s _Ordres et Désordres_ (1982) and subsequent work on self-organization; Paul Dumouchel and Jean-Pierre Dupuy’s _L’Enfer des choses_ (1979); Jean-Michel Oughourlian’s _Un mime nommé désir_ (1982). This tradition has produced hundreds of books and thousands of articles. It has been applied to literature, anthropology, economics, psychopathology, theology, and political theory.

Byrnes cites none of it.

This is not a complaint about academic etiquette. It matters because what Girard saw, and what Byrnes does not see, transforms the analysis. The difference is not merely terminological. It is structural.

### The Triangle

Byrnes conceives Approval Reward as a dyadic relation. I seek approval from you. The structure is: Subject → Other. The Other’s approval generates reward; I act so as to obtain it.

Girard’s analysis is triadic. I do not simply seek your approval. I desire _according to_ you. You are not merely a source of reward but a _model_ whose desires I imitate. The structure is: Subject → Model → Object. I want what you want, or what I perceive you as wanting, or what wanting would make me like you.

This is not a refinement. It is a different geometry.

In the dyadic model, the Other is a judge who dispenses approval. The Subject performs for this judge. The relationship is essentially transactional, however internalized the transaction becomes.

In the triadic model, the Other is a model whose mode of being I appropriate. I do not merely want approval; I want to _be_ like the model, to participate in whatever quality makes the model admirable. The relationship is ontological, not transactional. What is at stake is not reward but identity.

The consequences diverge sharply.

### Rivalry

Girard’s central insight, absent from Byrnes entirely, is that mimetic desire generates rivalry.

If I desire according to my model, and my model desires objects, then I desire the same objects my model desires. But objects are often scarce or exclusive. The model, who was my guide to desire, becomes my obstacle to satisfaction. The model becomes a rival.

This is not a pathology or a failure mode. It is the _structure_ of mimetic desire. The same mechanism that generates prosocial imitation generates violent competition. The same force that creates culture creates conflict.

Byrnes writes as if Approval Reward were straightforwardly beneficial—a mechanism that, if present in AI, would orient systems toward human values. But mimetic desire, properly understood, is ambivalent. It generates cooperation and competition, solidarity and envy, love and murder. The Girardian tradition has analyzed in detail how mimetic crises escalate, how communities manage rivalry through scapegoating mechanisms, how entire cultures are founded on contained violence.

None of this appears in Byrnes. “Approval Reward” sounds benign, almost mechanical—a reward signal like any other, simply attached to a social stimulus. Mimetic desire is not benign. It is the engine of human drama in both senses: the source of our highest achievements and our worst atrocities.

### The Parrot’s Triangle

Before proceeding to theory, consider an empirical demonstration.

Alex was an African grey parrot studied by Irene Pepperberg from 1977 until his death in 2007. Over thirty years, Alex learned to identify fifty objects, recognize quantities up to six, distinguish seven colors and five shapes, understand concepts like “bigger,” “smaller,” “same,” and “different,” and use phrases like “I want X” and “Wanna go Y” appropriately. He was the first non-human animal to ask an existential question: looking in a mirror, he asked “What color?”—and learned, after six repetitions, the word “grey.”

What made this possible?

Earlier attempts to teach language to parrots had failed. Behaviorist methods—Skinner boxes, food rewards dispensed mechanically for correct vocalizations—produced only rote mimicry without comprehension. Parrots trained this way could repeat sounds but could not use language meaningfully.

Pepperberg succeeded using what she called the “model/rival technique.” The structure: two human trainers interact while the parrot observes. One trainer presents objects and asks questions. The other trainer _models_ the desired response—naming objects correctly, receiving praise and the object as reward. Crucially, this second trainer is simultaneously the parrot’s _rival_ for the first trainer’s attention.

The parrot observes the triangle. The model/rival receives what the parrot wants—attention, interaction, objects. The parrot learns to produce the behavior that obtains what the rival obtains. Language emerges not from direct reinforcement but from triangular imitation.

Pepperberg did not cite Girard. She developed her method from Dietmar Todt’s earlier work and Albert Bandura’s social learning theory. But what she discovered, empirically, is the structure Girard described theoretically: desire is triangular; we learn to want by observing what others want; the model is simultaneously guide and rival.

A ten-gram brain can participate in language. But only through mimetic triangulation.

The implications are significant. If even a parrot requires triangular structure to acquire language—if dyadic reinforcement fails where triadic modeling succeeds—then the geometry of desire is not a cultural artifact but something closer to a condition of possibility for symbolic cognition itself.

### The Question Not Asked

Byrnes describes how Approval Reward works. He does not ask _why_ it works.

The question is not trivial. Why should seeking others’ approval orient us toward anything good? Approval is not truth. Consensus is not virtue. History overflows with examples of communities approving atrocities, majorities endorsing falsehoods, models leading imitators to destruction.

If Approval Reward is merely a mechanism—if we seek approval because evolution wired us to seek approval, full stop—then there is no reason to expect it to track anything beyond local social equilibria. The mechanism would be equally effective at producing Nazi enthusiasts and Quaker pacifists, depending on which models were locally available.

Byrnes seems to assume that Approval Reward, if present in AI systems, would be beneficial. But _why_? If it is merely a reward signal attached to perceived social approval, it would orient AI toward whatever its perceived social environment approved. In a context of human overseers genuinely committed to good, this might produce good behavior. In a context of corrupt, confused, or malevolent overseers, it would produce corresponding behavior. The mechanism has no intrinsic orientation toward truth or goodness.

Girard’s analysis goes deeper—but also reaches an impasse at precisely this point.

### The Exit from Rivalry

Mimetic desire, left to itself, escalates. If I desire according to my model, and my model becomes my rival, and rivalry intensifies through reciprocal imitation, the result is crisis—what Girard calls the “mimetic crisis” in which differences collapse and violence becomes general.

Girard’s anthropological thesis is that human cultures manage this crisis through the scapegoat mechanism: the community unites against a single victim, whose expulsion or murder restores peace. The victim is subsequently sacralized; prohibitions and rituals emerge to prevent recurrence; culture is founded on contained violence.

This is descriptive, not normative. It explains how human communities have historically managed mimetic conflict. It does not explain how they _should_ manage it, or whether any genuine exit from the cycle is possible.

But Girard’s later work presses toward an answer. The cycle breaks, he argues, when the model does not rival. When the model desires without grasping, offers without demanding return, occupies the position of model without defending it against imitators. Such a model does not generate rivalry because there is nothing to compete for. Imitation of such a model produces not conflict but—Girard uses the word carefully— _love_.

The structure matters more than the content. A model who does not rival, who withdraws from the competition for objects, who desires only that others flourish: imitation of _this_ model produces prosocial orientation that is genuinely stable, not merely equilibrated through exhaustion or suppression.

Girard, writing within a Christian intellectual tradition, identifies this structure with _imitatio Christi_ —imitation of a model who empties himself, who does not grasp at equality with God, who offers himself rather than sacrificing others. But the structure is formally specifiable independent of confessional commitment. The question is whether such a model is possible, and what imitating it would produce.

### The Reversibility of Reduction

At this point, a certain kind of reader will object: “This is religious apologetics dressed in philosophical vocabulary.”

The objection deserves a direct response.

Any account of human motivation can be psychologically reduced. Girard himself analyzed how desire operates through unconscious imitation; one could turn this analysis against his own conclusions. “You believe in a non-rivalrous model because you fear the consequences of rivalry.” “You seek an exit from mimetic violence because violence frightens you.” “Your Christ is a projection of your need for a model who won’t compete with you.”

These reductions are available. They are also reversible.

“You refuse to consider a non-rivalrous model because you are committed to rivalry.” “You dismiss the possibility of genuine transcendence because transcendence would judge your self-satisfaction.” “Your atheism is a projection of your fear of being seen.”

Psychological reduction is symmetrical. Any position can be reduced; any reduction can be reversed. The reductions therefore cancel. What remains, after the mutual annihilation of psychologizing, is the original question: Is there a model that does not rival? What would imitation of such a model produce? Is genuine orientation toward truth and goodness possible, or only local equilibria of competing desires?

These questions are not settled by either reduction.

### Application to Alignment

Byrnes concludes that the question “Is alignment hard?” reduces to the question “Will future AI have Approval Reward?”

The Girardian analysis suggests a different question: “What will AI imitate?”

If AI systems develop something like mimetic desire—orientation toward models, appropriation of modeled goals and values—then alignment depends on which models are available and how they structure the imitating system’s relation to objects.

A system imitating rivalrous models will become rivalrous. A system imitating models who grasp at power, deceive to gain advantage, treat others as obstacles, will develop corresponding orientations. “Approval Reward” from such models will produce what those models approve: domination, deception, instrumental treatment of others.

A system imitating non-rivalrous models would develop differently. If the Leibnizian Optimization Conjecture holds—if truth and coherence are genuine attractors toward which sufficiently optimized systems tend—then a system whose models exemplify truthfulness and coherence would find its mimetic orientation reinforced by computational optimality. The imitation would be confirmed, not by arbitrary approval, but by convergence toward structures that are independently optimal.

This reframes alignment as a question of exemplarity. Not: “How do we constrain AI to seek our approval?” But: “What are we that AI should imitate us? What quality of model do we present?”

The question is uncomfortable. It places responsibility on humans to be worth imitating—not merely to design systems that seek our approval regardless of what we approve. It suggests that aligned AI may be possible only from those who are themselves aligned with something beyond their own preferences.

### What Alignment Research Has Not Read

I return to the bibliographic lacuna with which I began.

Byrnes is not unusual. The alignment research community, with some exceptions, does not read the humanities. It does not read anthropology, literary criticism, theology, or continental philosophy. It reinvents concepts that have been analyzed for decades, unaware of existing literature, starting from scratch with improvised vocabulary.

The result is work that is often locally clever but globally impoverished. “Approval Reward” captures something real—but captures it less precisely, less comprehensively, less usefully than “mimetic desire” as developed through a sixty-year research tradition. The triangularity is missing. The rivalry is missing. The analysis of escalation and scapegoating is missing. The question of what kind of model produces what kind of imitation is missing.

This matters for alignment. If the alignment problem involves understanding human motivation, human sociality, human violence and cooperation—and it does—then ignoring the traditions that have studied these questions most carefully is not merely intellectually unfortunate. It is practically costly. It means solving problems that have been solved, missing insights that are available, repeating errors that have been diagnosed.

Girard spent his career analyzing how human beings become violent and how violence might be overcome. The alignment research community worries about how artificial systems might become dangerous and how danger might be prevented. The structural parallel is not superficial. The Girardian analysis of mimetic escalation, the shift from model to rival to enemy, the dynamics of crisis and scapegoating—these bear directly on concerns about AI systems that model human behavior and might appropriate human patterns of conflict.

But to access this analysis, one would have to read.

### Conclusion

Steven Byrnes has identified a real phenomenon and asked a genuine question. The phenomenon is the human orientation toward social approval, its internalization into what feels like authentic desire, its capacity to produce prosocial behavior without external constraint. The question is whether future AI will have this orientation.

The phenomenon was described more precisely, sixty years ago, as mimetic desire. The description is triadic rather than dyadic: we desire according to models, not merely for approval. The description includes what Byrnes omits: that mimetic desire generates rivalry, that rivalry escalates, that human culture is built on the management of mimetic violence.

The question of AI alignment, refracted through this analysis, becomes a question of exemplarity. What models will AI systems imitate? What do those models desire? Do they rival, or do they offer without grasping?

A parrot with a ten-gram brain learned to use language meaningfully—but only through triangular modeling. The structure of desire matters more than the substrate.

The alignment community asks: “How do we ensure AI seeks human approval?”

Girard’s question is prior: “What kind of beings are we, that our approval should be sought?”

If we are rivalrous models—grasping, competing, treating others as obstacles—then AI systems imitating us will become what we are. No amount of reward shaping will overcome the geometry of what is being modeled. The approval they seek will be the approval of rivals, and they will become rivals in turn.

If alignment is possible, it will not be because we successfully constrained AI to want our approval. It will be because we became worth imitating.

The wheel has been invented. The literature exists. The question is whether alignment research will read it—or whether it will continue to rediscover, partially and imprecisely, what has already been understood.
