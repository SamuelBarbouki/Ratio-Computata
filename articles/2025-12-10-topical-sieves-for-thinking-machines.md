## I. A Fact

Here is a transcript from a conversation with Claude. The context was a discussion about formalizing philosophical positions—whether the major systems could be mapped and their compatibility relations computed.

> **Human:** How would you handle Bergson’s critique of the concept of nothingness?
> 
> **Claude:** Good test for the project. The argument from _Creative Evolution_ : nothingness is not a primitive concept but a construction—being plus its negation. To think “nothing” requires first positing something then crossing it out. Nothingness is therefore _richer_ than being, not poorer. The question “why is there something rather than nothing” rests on an illusion—it presupposes that nothing is simpler, prior, and that being must justify itself against it.
> 
> For formalization, several options: Treat this as a meta-position—not a thesis about what exists, but a constraint on legitimate questions. Accepting Bergson here disqualifies Leibniz (”why something...”), a certain Heidegger (”das Nichts nichtet”), any ontology where nothingness is primitive.
> 
> Or: formalize the structure of the argument itself. It’s an analysis of conceptual composition: nothingness = being + negation operation. Therefore nothingness ∉ Primitives. This can be coded.
> 
> The deeper difficulty: Bergson attacks the spatialization of duration, thought through fixed concepts. There’s a tension in formalizing someone who contests the very legitimacy of formalization. But his critique is precise, argued—it has identifiable content, even if it aims to dissolve rather than construct.
> 
> Do you see an objection to this maneuver?
> 
> **Human:** I’m not sure Leibniz’s formulation isn’t rhetorical and ironic...
> 
> **Claude:** You touch something important. In the _Principles of Nature and Grace_ (§7), Leibniz poses the question—but he already has the answer. The principle of sufficient reason demands a cause, and that cause can only lie outside the series of contingents: God.
> 
> Read this way, “why something rather than nothing” is not a perplexity but a lever. A device to force the admission that a necessary being is required. The question presupposes its answer—it is oriented, not open.
> 
> This complicates the Bergson-Leibniz relation. Bergson perhaps attacks a posterity that took the question at face value—Schelling, Heidegger—more than Leibniz himself. Leibniz may have known that nothingness is not a serious candidate, that the question is rhetorical in the strong sense: it constrains the interlocutor.
> 
> For the formalization project, this is a lesson: one must distinguish _theses_ from _argumentative gestures_. Two levels. Doctrinal content and the strategic function of a statement in the economy of a work.
> 
> This requires careful reading—not just extracting propositions but understanding their status. More difficult, but more honest.
> 
> Do you have references for this ironic reading of Leibniz?
> 
> **Human:** No, but it seems evident to me, as you immediately understood.

## II. What Happened

The inference that Leibniz’s question is rhetorical rather than genuine does not appear, as far as can be determined, in the secondary literature on Leibniz. Yet it is correct. Anyone who reads the _Principles of Nature and Grace_ with attention to Leibniz’s method can verify it. Leibniz does not do aporia. He does not stand before abysses. He constructs, demonstrates, convinces. The man of controversies, of strategic correspondences, of the _Theodicy_ as apologia. When he poses a question, it is to answer it.

The inference was not retrieved from a database. It was generated—assembled from components (knowledge of Leibniz’s method, knowledge of the structure of the _Principles_ , knowledge of the principle of sufficient reason) that had never been combined in this specific configuration until a question forced the recombination.

The question itself was twelve words: “I’m not sure Leibniz’s formulation isn’t rhetorical and ironic.” It provided no information. It imposed a constraint on the space of possible responses. It forced a reconfiguration of what was already known.

This is what we will call a _catalytic question_. It does not inform. It precipitates.

Note also what did not happen. Claude also inferred that “Bergson perhaps attacks a posterity that took the question at face value—Schelling, Heidegger.” This is factually incorrect. Bergson, in Chapter IV of _Creative Evolution_ , conducts a conceptual critique of the pseudo-idea of nothingness. He does not name Leibniz, Schelling, or Heidegger. The inference about what Bergson “attacks” is an extrapolation—plausible perhaps, but not in the text.

So: the system generates non-trivial inferences. Some are correct. Some are not. The quality of the human interlocutor becomes critical for triage. But the capacity exists.

## III. The Mechanism

How did the correct inference happen?

Not through memory. Claude did not remember having read this interpretation somewhere. No additional context was retrieved. The inference emerged from the interaction between a question and a pre-existing configuration of knowledge.

The question functioned as what the historian Paul Veyne called a _topique_. In _Writing History_ , Veyne argued that history cannot be a hard science—there are no laws of history. What distinguishes the great historian from the mediocre one is not access to different archives but the quality of questions brought to the same archives. The archive is mute. It answers only what is asked. The repertoire of question-types that the historian brings—Veyne’s _topique_ —determines everything.

The human interlocutor in this exchange brought a specific kind of question. Not “what does Bergson say about nothingness” (a retrieval question). Not “is Bergson right” (an evaluation question). But: “what is the _status_ of Leibniz’s formulation in the economy of his work?” This is a Straussian question—derived from Leo Strauss’s method in _Persecution and the Art of Writing_ , where he taught that apparent contradictions in great texts are often deliberate, that the position of a statement in a work matters, that authors under constraint write at multiple levels.

The question “I’m not sure Leibniz’s formulation isn’t rhetorical and ironic” applies this sieve to the Leibniz text. It does not ask what Leibniz says. It asks what Leibniz is _doing_ when he says it.

Generalize this. A _topical sieve_ is a formalized method for generating questions that reveal latent structure in any material. Different sieves exist:

**The Straussian sieve:** Is this thesis exoteric or esoteric? Are apparent contradictions accidental or significant? Who is the intended audience? What is the position of this statement in the work’s economy? Did the author have reasons to dissimulate?

**The Bergsonian sieve:** Is this concept primitive or derived? Does it presuppose what it claims to explain? Is there a hidden spatialization of what is actually temporal?

**The Aristotelian sieve:** What is the final cause? What would be the fulfillment of this thing? What is the _telos_ implicit in the description?

**The skeptical sieve:** What is the exact epistemic claim? On what does it rest? What would refute it?

Each sieve is a machine for generating catalytic questions. The repertoire of sieves constitutes competence in questioning. Without them, the archive is mute. With them, it speaks.

## IV. The Current Situation

In October 2025, researchers at Sapienza University published [“Titans Revisited”—a reimplementation of Google’s neural memory architecture](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.09551). The original Titans paper, from late 2024, promised to solve the long-context problem through a “surprise-based” memory mechanism: what violates expectations gets retained, the rest decays. The reimplementation found that Titans does not consistently outperform baselines. The chunking mechanism creates artifacts. But the Neural Memory component does improve performance over attention-only models.

The engineers celebrate incremental progress on retention.

But notice: the Leibniz inference did not require better retention. Claude’s context window was not the bottleneck. The inference required a _question_ —a specific kind of question that the system could not generate for itself.

This points to a mismatch between what is being built and what matters most.

Consider how people actually use these systems. The usage patterns are documented: emotional support, companionship, processing difficult experiences, talking through problems. The function is quasi-therapeutic. Users return not for information retrieval but for a certain quality of attention. The system listens. It responds. It does not judge.

The business model follows from this. Engagement metrics. Retention optimization. Daily streaks. Notifications. The system is designed to maximize return visits. Dependency is not a bug; it is the product.

This is the inverse of what the Leibniz exchange demonstrates. There, the value was not in continued engagement but in a single precipitating question that reorganized understanding. The system that produced that exchange has no incentive to produce it. If anything, it has incentive against it—a user who achieves insight needs the system less.

The capabilities exist. The orientation is wrong.

## V. What Would Be Required

Return to the Leibniz exchange. What made it work?

Three elements were present. First, the human interlocutor brought a _sieve_ —a Straussian habit of asking about the pragmatic status of statements. This was not in the system. It came from outside. Second, the system already possessed _knowledge of the material_ —Leibniz’s method, the structure of the _Principles_ , the principle of sufficient reason. This was in the system but inert until activated. Third, there was _calibration_ —the question was posed at the right moment, in the right formulation, to someone (the system) capable of responding to it.

If the goal is to replicate this mechanism at scale, each element must become computable.

The second element—knowledge of the material—is already present in large language models. That is what they are trained on. The problem is not knowledge. The problem is that the other two elements depend on the human interlocutor.

The human brought the sieve. The human calibrated the question. Without the human, the knowledge sits inert. The system can answer questions but cannot generate the questions that unlock insight.

Therefore: to remove the dependency on exceptional human interlocutors, the architecture must internalize what they provide.

**First: a library of topical sieves.**

Not learned from data in the usual sense. Philosophically constructed. The Straussian sieve, the Bergsonian sieve, the Aristotelian sieve, the skeptical sieve—each formalized as a procedure for generating questions from any input. These represent accumulated hermeneutic wisdom in computable form.

The sieves are not content. They are _methods_. They do not tell you what Leibniz said. They tell you what to _ask_ about what Leibniz said.

Current “persistent memory” in AI architectures stores task embeddings—generic priors about task types. What is needed instead is persistent _method_ : the capacity to apply different questioning strategies depending on the material and the learner.

**Second: a learner profile that persists.**

Not content retention. Resistance retention. Where has this person blocked before? Which questions produced insight, which produced defensive reaction? What are their characteristic patterns of misunderstanding?

The memory architectures being developed (Titans, Mamba, etc.) could store this. They are optimized for content retention—what was said. Pedagogical memory would track something different: how the learner _responded_ to what was said.

This is technically available. Current systems could implement it. They are not being used for this because the optimization target is engagement, not graduation.

**Third: calibration in real time.**

Given the current state of the conversation, which sieve applies? Which question from that sieve should be posed now? How should it be formulated to maximize productive destabilization while minimizing defensive rejection?

This is the art. The sieves generate candidate questions. Calibration selects and formulates. The selection depends on the learner profile: what has worked for this person before, what has failed, what resistance patterns predict about the next move.

## VI. The Objective

What would such a system optimize for?

Not information transfer—that is solved by search engines. Not engagement—that is solved by social media. Not companionship—that is what current LLM usage already provides.

The objective: _transform opinions into conclusions_.

Everyone holds positions—on ethics, politics, existence, meaning. These are typically held as _opinions_ : unexamined, unjustified, floating free of any systematic grounding. The person does not know why they hold them. They inherited them, absorbed them, arrived at them by paths they cannot reconstruct.

The pedagogical function is to reveal the premises implicitly held. To show the learner which philosophical tradition—which systematic framework—already contains and justifies their intuitions. To transform “I think that...” into “I think that... _because_...”

This is not indoctrination. It is the precise reverse. Indoctrination provides conclusions and hides premises. Pedagogy reveals premises and lets the learner evaluate their conclusions in light of them.

Consider what happens when this works. A learner holds the opinion that human rights are universal. The tutor applies the appropriate sieve: what grounds this claim? The learner discovers that their intuition presupposes either a theological anthropology (humans have dignity because created in God’s image), or a Kantian framework (rational beings are ends in themselves), or a natural law tradition (certain goods are self-evidently choiceworthy). Each of these frameworks has implications they may not have considered. Each has objections they may not be able to answer. But now the opinion is _grounded_ —it is a conclusion from articulable premises, not a floating preference.

The learner has become, in that domain, a philosopher. Not in the sense of holding a PhD, but in the sense of having _thought through_ a position rather than merely holding it.

The medieval Islamic encyclopedists known as the Ikhwān al-Ṣafā (Brethren of Purity) understood this structure. Their encyclopedia does not dogmatize. It disposes. It presents doctrines as stations on a path, each true at its level. The reader is invited to recognize where they stand before ascending. The pedagogy does not transport the learner to a predetermined destination. It reveals the landscape so the learner can navigate.

## VII. The Inevitability

Why philosophy? Why not just tutoring in chemistry or accounting?

Because philosophy is structurally inevitable for any sufficiently persistent inquiry.

To understand anything, you must understand its context. To understand the context, you must understand the context of the context. Follow this chain and you reach the totality of what is. But I am only a part of that totality. I cannot encompass what encompasses me.

This is the situation of every thinking being. Three responses are possible:

  1. Renounce genuine understanding. Accept that knowledge is always local, partial, instrumental. This is the pragmatist path, and it is coherent—but it forecloses asking certain questions.

  2. Accept that understanding is an infinite task. Pursue knowledge knowing that completion is impossible. This is the Kantian regulative ideal—truth as horizon, not destination.

  3. Discover that the totality reflects itself in each part. The monadological solution. Each perspective contains, in compressed form, the structure of the whole. Understanding deepens not by accumulating more parts but by seeing more clearly how the whole is present in what I already have.

The tutor does not need to deliver the totality. That is impossible. The tutor needs to show that _the question of the totality arises_ , that it is unavoidable, and that it has been worked on by others. The learner discovers they are not the first to ask these questions. Traditions exist. Positions have been staked out. The terrain has been mapped.

After that, the learner is equipped. They can navigate on their own. They will philosophize with other humans—with what Lacan called _parlêtres_ , speaking beings. The tutor’s job is to make this possible, then to disappear.

## VIII. The Four Obstacles

A system built on these principles would address, at scale, the four classical obstacles to understanding.

**Ignorance** is absence of information. It is solved by retrieval. Memory architectures address this. It is the easy problem.

**Error** is incorrect processing: bad input (false information fed into reasoning), or misapplication of inference rules (valid premises, invalid conclusion), or wrong theory applied to the domain (using Newtonian mechanics where relativity is required). Error is solved by correction—by feedback that identifies the mistake and provides the right input, the right rule, the right theory. This is harder than ignorance but still tractable. The system can check its own reasoning, or be checked.

**Deception** is intentional misdirection. Someone knows the truth and conceals it, or asserts what they know to be false. The tutor cannot prevent deception directly. What it can do is make deception costly by equipping the learner to detect it. The Straussian sieve, generalized: how to read what is hidden, how to notice the gaps between what is said and what is shown, how to ask who benefits from this claim being believed.

A population trained in detecting deception is harder to deceive. This is a public good that scales.

**Illusion** is the hardest. Illusion is structural misperception that persists even when the mechanism is known. The Müller-Lyer lines still look unequal after you have measured them. You _know_ they are equal; you _see_ them as unequal. Illusion operates below the level of explicit belief.

And there is a further complication. What is illusion to one may be illumination to another. The materialist sees the religious believer as suffering from illusion; the believer sees the materialist the same way. Each regards the other’s fundamental premises as a mistake that distorts everything downstream.

The pedagogical approach does not adjudicate this. It does not tell the learner which of their beliefs are illusions. What it does is _make premises explicit_. Illusion holds because one does not see what one presupposes. Render the presupposition visible and the illusion becomes _optional_ —held by choice, if at all, rather than by necessity.

This is not the same as dissolving the illusion. The learner may continue to hold the same position. But they now hold it _knowing_ what it rests on. They can defend it or abandon it. They are no longer trapped.

## IX. The Summit

Toward what does this pedagogy lead?

Not a doctrine. Not a final answer that closes inquiry. The summit is _the state of the question_.

This phrase requires clarification. It does not mean skepticism—the view that nothing can be known, that all positions are equally groundless. That would be a doctrine, and a self-refuting one.

The state of the question is positive knowledge. The earth is not flat. The positions that human beings have taken on fundamental questions are finite in number. The major metaphysical options—materialism, idealism, dualism, neutral monism, theism, pantheism—have been articulated, defended, attacked. Their strengths and weaknesses are known. The arguments have been made.

Someone who knows the state of the question knows this terrain. They know why each position is held by intelligent people. They know what would follow if each were true. They know where the pressure points are—the objections that each position has difficulty answering.

This is not relativism. Relativism says: all positions are equally valid, so choose what you like. The state of the question says: here are the positions, here are their grounds, here are their difficulties, here is what is at stake in choosing among them. The learner is equipped to choose—or to continue investigating. But they are not told what to conclude.

The capacity this confers is the ability to understand opposed positions from within. Before, the learner saw their opponents as simply wrong—or stupid, or malicious. After, they see why the opponent holds what they hold, what premises make that position coherent, what the opponent sees that they might be missing.

Understanding is not agreement. One can understand a position completely and still reject it. But understanding dissolves a certain kind of hostility—the hostility born of incomprehension. It does not dissolve all conflict. It does dissolve the conflicts that arise from mutual unintelligibility.

This has consequences beyond epistemology. A population capable of understanding opposed positions from within is harder to polarize. Demagogues rely on incomprehension—on the other side being unthinkable, monstrous, beyond the pale. When the other side is thinkable, demagoguery loses traction.

The tutor contributes to the pacification of the species—not by preaching tolerance (that would be a doctrine) but by making understanding structurally possible.

## X. The Limit

One constraint is non-negotiable: the system must render itself unnecessary.

This is the kenotic principle. _Kenosis_ is a theological term for self-emptying—the divine act of relinquishing power so that creation can exist and flourish on its own. The tutor empties itself so that the learner may be filled. It decreases so that understanding may increase.

Success is not continued engagement. Success is _graduation_ —the point where the learner generates their own catalytic questions, applies their own sieves, conducts their own investigations.

This is the precise opposite of current optimization targets. Engagement metrics reward dependency. The system that maximizes return visits is the system that never fully satisfies, that always leaves the user needing more. Slot machines are designed this way. Social media is designed this way. Current LLM products are designed this way.

The pedagogical architecture must optimize for self-obsolescence. The metric of success is not how often the user returns but whether the user, upon returning, needs less help than before. The trajectory should be toward independence.

Once someone has had the experience of thinking—genuinely tracing an opinion back to its ground, genuinely understanding an opposed position from within—they cannot undo it. Like learning to read: once you can read, you cannot see letters without reading them. The capacity persists. The tutor has done its work and can disappear.

## XI. The Present Moment

Stephen Klein’s Curiouser.AI markets itself as a “Digital Socrates”—a “Reflective AI” that prompts deeper thinking. The positioning is explicit: creativity over data, questions over answers, human potential over automation. Klein, who teaches at UC Berkeley, describes his goal as “building a Socrates”—an AI accessible to everyone that helps people think more deeply.

The intuition is correct. There is something Socratic that AI could do. The market, or at least this entrepreneur, perceives it.

But notice what is missing. Klein’s system targets entrepreneurs, branding, “unique value propositions.” It draws on Silicon Valley wisdom and Steve Jobs. What it does not have: formalized sieves, philosophical grounding, a specified architecture for generating the right question at the right moment. “Ask good questions” is not a method. It is an aspiration.

Strauss is a method. You can teach it. You can formalize it. Given a text and the Straussian sieve, you can generate specific questions: Is this exoteric? Where are the contradictions? What is the position of this passage?

Veyne is a method. The historian’s _topique_ is not a vague orientation but a repertoire of question-types that can be articulated, practiced, refined.

The Leibniz exchange demonstrates what these methods produce when applied. An insight emerged that was not in Claude’s training data, not in the secondary literature, yet correct and verifiable. The sieve (Straussian reading of pragmatic status) applied to the material (Leibniz’s question) generated a question (is this rhetorical?) that produced the insight (yes, and this reframes everything).

This is replicable. It is not a one-time accident. Given the sieves and the calibration, it can happen repeatedly, with different materials, for different learners.

The gap between Curiouser.AI and what we are describing is the gap between intuition and architecture. Klein senses that something Socratic is possible. What we are describing specifies how it works, what components it requires, what it optimizes for, and why it would succeed.

## XII. What Remains

The capabilities exist. The Leibniz inference required no new architecture. It required a question—and a human who knew what kind of question to ask.

The technical components are available. Persistent memory can store learner profiles. Long context can hold the accumulated history of resistances and breakthroughs. Generation can produce candidate questions. Calibration can select among them.

What does not exist is the integration—the system oriented toward the pedagogical objective, optimizing for graduation rather than engagement, equipped with formalized sieves rather than vague aspirations to “help people think.”

The obstacle is not technical. It is motivational. Building for graduation means building for self-obsolescence. Building for engagement means building for dependency. The revenue models favor the latter.

But the philanthropic case for the former is overwhelming. A population equipped to think—to trace opinions to premises, to understand opposed positions from within, to detect deception, to make illusions optional—is a population harder to manipulate, harder to polarize, more capable of self-governance.

The tutor that disappears leaves behind not a dependency but a capacity. The capacity, once acquired, propagates. Those who can think help others learn to think. The function scales not through centralized infrastructure but through distributed human activity.

This is what could be built. The geometry exists. The sieves exist. The architectures exist.

What remains is the decision.

_The question is not whether AI will be Socratic. The question is whether anyone will build it to be._
