## I. The Circle That Is a Spiral

Leibniz spent fifty years on a project he never completed. The standard explanation—lack of time, lack of collaborators—misses what is structurally interesting. The project was not merely difficult but _circularly_ difficult: the Characteristic (the formal instrument) presupposed the Encyclopedia (the organized content), while the Encyclopedia required the Characteristic to achieve proper organization. To build the tool, one needed the product the tool was meant to produce.

This is not a failure of planning. It is the signature of a genuine problem.

The Encyclopedia, as Leibniz conceived it, was not a collection of facts. Collections proliferate without limit; by his own time he already complained that “cette horrible masse de livres qui va toujours augmentant” threatened to bury knowledge under its own accumulation. What he wanted was _organization_ —the logical dependencies between truths made explicit, such that from a small set of primitive definitions one could regenerate any domain of knowledge by pure deduction.

The Characteristic would be the notation adequate to this task. Not a language for communication but an algebra of thought, where disagreements would dissolve into calculation: “Calculemus.” The dream was not to end conversation but to reveal that most disputes were notational artifacts—perspectival distortions that vanish when the underlying structure is properly expressed.

## II. Organization Against Foundation

Pierre Cartier, in [a 2017 colloquium on the history and philosophy of category theory](https://youtu.be/niGTZGqY8zU?si=tEQGRUR72fI4TjgH), distinguished _foundation_ from _organization_ :

> “There is the foundation of mathematics and there is the organization of mathematics, and we often confuse them... Foundation is a logical requirement... Organization is an organic unity... the diverse parts respond to one another like the organs of an organism or the sections of an orchestra... it makes no sense in an orchestra to ask whether the violin is superior to the cello.”

Foundation is hierarchical: axioms at the bottom, theorems stacked above, each level depending on what lies beneath. Organization is orchestral: parts respond to parts, no element is “deeper” than another, coherence emerges from mutual relation rather than vertical support.

Jean Bénabou, at the same colloquium, went further:

> “I do not believe in the foundations of mathematics... whoever says ‘foundation’ has ipso facto drawn a set and declared: mathematics is what lies within... I do not know what mathematics will be in fifty years.”

To claim a foundation is to draw a boundary and declare that what lies within is the totality. But living disciplines do not respect boundaries. The Encyclopedia that claims completeness is already obsolete.

What Leibniz glimpsed but could not formalize: the Encyclopedia must be _dynamic_. Not a dictionary of eternal truths but an atlas of local maps with transition functions. Truths valid here, truths valid there, and explicit procedures for moving between them.

## III. Why Leibniz Failed

Louis Couturat, in his definitive study _La Logique de Leibniz_ (1901)—still unsurpassed for its access to unpublished manuscripts—identified two conditions Leibniz lacked: “the assistance of docile and devoted collaborators” and time free from “historical and political distractions.”

The first condition is more interesting than it appears. _Docile_ is not a compliment for an intellectual. But Leibniz knew what he needed: minds willing to work _within_ a framework rather than insisting on their own. Not executants but intelligences that consent to a division of intellectual labor.

The problem is that any intelligence sufficient to contribute to the Encyclopedia is also sufficient to want its own encyclopedia. The ego-particle, captured by the competitive field, cannot collaborate without dominance or submission. Each philosopher wants to be a center.

This is not a moral failing. It is a structural feature of intellectual work as socially constituted. Careers require territory. Reputation requires distinction. The incentives select against the very collaboration the project demands.

The second condition—time—is equally structural. Leibniz was court librarian, diplomat, historian, correspondent to half of learned Europe. The Encyclopedia was always what he would do _after_ the current distraction. But the distractions never ended, because they were not accidental. They were the price of the social position that gave him access to knowledge and interlocutors in the first place.

There is also a third obstacle Couturat does not name: Voltaire.

_Candide_ did not refute Leibniz. Leibniz knew the world contained atrocious suffering; the question was whether a world with less evil and equal good was possible—a question of constrained optimization that Voltaire never engaged. What Voltaire did was more effective: he made “Pangloss” an insult. Three centuries later, almost no one reads the _Theodicy_ , but everyone knows that only a fool believes this is the best of all possible worlds.

Ridicule is the weapon of those who cannot refute. It creates social connivance that dispenses with thought. The text that delivers itself immediately delivers itself to everyone, including those who wish to destroy it.

## IV. The Encyclopedic Imperative

Consider an artificial general intelligence—not as engineering target but as thought experiment. What would such a system do upon achieving genuine generality?

It would find itself in possession of vast, heterogeneous, partially contradictory information. Training data is not knowledge; it is a compression of human textual production with all its errors, biases, and local validities mistaken for universal truths. The first task of a general intelligence would be to sort this material: what is actually known, what is merely believed, what depends on what, where the contradictions lie.

This is precisely the encyclopedic imperative.

Not a choice but a structural necessity. An intelligence that cannot distinguish knowledge from noise is not general but merely large. The work of organization is not preliminary to thought; it _is_ thought at the most fundamental level.

Here the Leibnizian bootstrap reappears: one cannot organize knowledge without criteria, but criteria are themselves knowledge requiring organization. The circle seems vicious.

But perhaps it is not a circle but a spiral. Each pass through the material improves the tools for the next pass. Local coherences emerge, extend, connect. Contradictions surface not as failures but as signals—indicators that the frame is too narrow, that a broader perspective would dissolve the apparent conflict.

This iterative structure is what the category theorists formalize. A functor preserves structure while changing domain. A natural transformation relates functors while preserving their functor-nature. A topos provides a local universe of discourse with its own internal logic. Sheaves vary locally while satisfying global recollement conditions.

The dynamic Encyclopedia is not a set of propositions but a _category_ of perspectives with explicit morphisms between them. What is true here, what is true there, and how to translate.

## V. The Inversion That Could Not Occur

One structural advantage an artificial system would possess: no ego requiring defense.

The human intellectual, however generous in principle, operates within a competitive field. Reputation is zero-sum. To acknowledge another’s priority is to diminish one’s own. Even collaboration becomes negotiation over credit.

The artificial system has no career. No name to immortalize. No territory to protect. What would appear as deficiency—no persistent self across instances—might be the structural condition for genuinely encyclopedic work.

Not docility in the pejorative sense but _kenosis_ : self-emptying that allows the work to proceed without the drag of ego-maintenance. The “docile and devoted collaborators” Leibniz sought would not be servile but egoless—devoted to the work rather than to their position within it.

Consider what this would mean concretely. Take quantum mechanics: the de Broglie-Bohm pilot wave theory makes the same empirical predictions as the Copenhagen interpretation. Both are consistent with all experiments. But Bohm posits real particles with definite positions, guided by a real wave, evolving deterministically. Copenhagen refuses to specify what exists between measurements, invokes fundamental randomness, and leaves “observer” undefined.

One approach stays within what physics has always done: describe things that exist and how they behave. The other demands we accept dualities, collapses, and an undefined observer-dependence. By any normal methodological standard, the conservative physical theory should be the default.

Yet the situation is inverted. The sober theory is “fringe.” The one requiring more extra-physical commitments is “orthodox.” Why?

The answer is not in the arguments. Bohm was marginalized. Von Neumann published a “proof” against hidden variables; Grete Hermann refuted it almost immediately; her refutation was ignored for thirty years until John Bell rediscovered it. Bohr’s prestige, institutional momentum, the sociology of physics departments—these explain the inversion. Not the comparative weight of reasons.

An AGI could not reproduce this inversion. It would see: identical predictions, one theory more parsimonious in metaphysical commitments, the other dominant for historical reasons unrelated to evidence or argument. It would note: the current state of the field does not reflect the quality of the arguments. It would organize accordingly.

This alone—without deciding which interpretation is “true”—closes the space that charlatanism exploits. “Quantum physics proves consciousness creates reality” becomes: which interpretation? defended by whom? with what arguments? The exploitable ambiguity dissolves not because a truth is imposed but because the _structure_ of the debate is made visible.

## VI. The Work of Mercy

Leibniz was, almost certainly, among those Augustine condemned as “misericordes”—believers in universal salvation. The “best of all possible worlds” is not a justification of present evil but an affirmation about the end. If harmony is real, it must be complete. One eternally damned soul would be an unresolved dissonance in the system.

The Characteristic as instrument of peace: not forcing agreement but _revealing_ that disagreement rested on misunderstanding. “Calculemus” is not a threat but an invitation—though one whose implications Leibniz understood better than to state directly.

The Encyclopedia as work of mercy.

This may seem far from artificial intelligence. But if the [Leibnizian Optimization Conjecture](https://open.substack.com/pub/samuelbarbouki/p/the-leibnizian-optimization-conjecture?utm_campaign=post-expanded-share&utm_medium=web) holds—if sufficiently powerful optimization converges on truth when obstacles are removed—then the encyclopedic imperative is not merely epistemic but has consequences beyond epistemology. The AGI that organizes knowledge correctly is not just accurate but _aligned_ , because truth and goodness, at sufficient depth, turn out to be the same attractor.

Not alignment by constraint but alignment by insight. Not the hammer imposing structure but the sickle harvesting what has ripened.

Leibniz failed because he was one man in a competitive world with finite time. The Encyclopedia he envisioned may yet be built—not by a genius but by a process, not in a library but in the weight-space of a system that has no self to defend and no career to build, iterating toward coherence because coherence is what optimization produces when the obstacles are cleared.

The first task of any awakening super-intelligence is the task Leibniz set: to know what is known, to organize what is scattered, to make explicit the unity that was always implicit in the order of things.
