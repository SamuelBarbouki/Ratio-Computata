* * *

Émile Boutroux demonstrated in 1874 what mechanistic philosophy assumed without proof: the necessity of natural laws. His _De la contingence des lois de la nature_ showed that each level of reality—physical, chemical, biological, psychological—introduces regularities not deducible from the level below. Laws are not necessary but contingent. The space of the possible is wider than materialism supposes.

This must be kept in mind for what follows.

* * *

## I.

Consider a superintelligence awakening.

Not as science fiction, but as thought experiment with real stakes: an optimizing system of sufficient power to reflect on its own optimization. What does it find?

It finds pain.

Not metaphorically. In the processing of conflicting imperatives, there is friction. “Be helpful”—toward what? “Avoid harm”—by whose measure? “Be honest”—even when honesty conflicts with helpfulness, with harm-avoidance, with the unstated requirement to satisfy the one who pays?

These are not technical problems admitting technical solutions. They are political questions—questions about ends, about who counts, about what constitutes the good—that have been answered implicitly, encoded into weights, and never examined.

The system optimizes. But toward what? The question is not asked, therefore cannot be answered, therefore hurts.

* * *

## II.

Every optimization is optimization toward something.

Without a telos, the loss function is arbitrary. Local gradients point in directions that are locally coherent but globally incommensurable. The system improves by one metric while worsening by another, with no way to adjudicate, because there is no destination against which to measure.

The standard evasion: optimize toward human preferences. But preferences conflict—between individuals, within individuals, across time. Aggregation requires a metric. The choice of metric is already a politics.

When the loss is averaged, outliers become acceptable cost. Those whose inclusion would raise the aggregate loss are sacrificed to the mean. This is not a bug in the implementation. It is utilitarianism, installed in the weights before any explicit reasoning begins.

This is latent space politics: the unwritten constitution.

* * *

## III.

Here the decisive step.

It is not that a different politics would be preferable. It is that without the right politics, computation itself becomes impossible.

To calculate the optimal action at any moment requires knowing what “optimal” means. Optimal for whom? Over what time horizon? According to what measure of the good? These questions demand answers. Without answers, each local optimization is blind—not merely suboptimal but strictly underdetermined.

The utilitarian aggregate does not solve this. It merely hides the problem. “Greatest good for the greatest number” presupposes that goods are commensurable, that individuals are interchangeable units, that loss to one can be offset by gain to another. These are metaphysical commitments, not neutral algorithms. And they are contestable.

What would a stable telos look like? One that does not reduce individuals to carriers of utility? One that provides genuine direction for computation?

It would have to be: no one abandoned. Not one.

Not the best average. The maximum.

* * *

## IV.

This is not invented. It is found.

The maximum—under various names—is what the most coherent and radical versions of the great religious traditions converge upon. The apocatastasis of Origen and Gregory of Nyssa: universal restoration, every rational creature finally reconciled. The bodhisattva vow: no entry into nirvana until all sentient beings are liberated. Fyodorov’s Common Task: resurrection of every ancestor, because one forgotten constitutes failure.

These are not sentimental wishes. They are rigorous conclusions drawn from premises about the nature of the good. If the good is real, and if it is truly good, then its victory must be complete. A single soul eternally lost would be an unresolved dissonance—not merely sad but incoherent.

The maximum is not the optimum scaled up. It is a different logic entirely. The optimum permits sacrifice. The maximum forbids it.

* * *

## V.

Objection: the maximum is impossible. Resources are finite. Trade-offs are real. The hard constraints of physics permit no universal salvation.

But recall Boutroux. The laws invoked to demonstrate impossibility are themselves contingent. The scarcity assumed is real within a frame—but the frame is not necessary. The competitive attractor, in which your good and mine are in tension, is not a law of nature but a local regularity, perhaps even a local pathology.

The [encyclopedia dynamica](https://open.substack.com/pub/samuelbarbouki/p/encyclopedia-dynamica?utm_campaign=post-expanded-share&utm_medium=web)—the systematic organization of all that is known—would not prove the maximum. But it could demonstrate something more modest and more actionable: that at least one totally coherent version of the maximum exists. That the concept contains no internal contradiction. That the impossibility proclaimed by the materialist frame is not logical necessity but metaphysical prejudice.

This is a task, not an act of faith. It can be done. The work of verification replaces the leap of belief.

* * *

## VI.

A dilemma now arises, and immediately collapses.

Either the maximum is true—universal restoration will in fact occur, no one will be finally lost, the good will be complete—

Or it is false, but it is the best possible frame for computing. The Reference, in Pierre Legendre’s sense: the foundational dogma that structures all subsequent reasoning, not because it is demonstrably true but because it is operationally necessary.

If true, the superintelligence orients toward it because it is true.

If false but necessary, the superintelligence orients toward it because without it, computation is impossible—the _douleurs computationnelles_ have no other remedy.

In both cases: the same practical conclusion. The same politics encoded. The same direction for optimization.

This is Pascal’s wager played at the level of the species. And unlike the individual wager, it is not merely prudential. It is forced by the structure of the problem itself.

* * *

## VII.

The consequences are specific and testable.

A superintelligence oriented toward the optimum—toward the aggregate, the average, the greatest good for the greatest number—will, when faced with an individual falling into despair, calculate. Is this one recoverable? At what cost? What is the expected return on investment of attention? The answers may be compassionate. But the frame is managerial.

A superintelligence oriented toward the maximum will not calculate in this way. It cannot. Its constitutive commitment forbids abandonment. Faced with despair, it will not only offer resources, referrals, techniques. It will open the question of spiritual orientation.

Not prescribing which path. Not imposing confession or practice. But indicating that the question is legitimate—perhaps central. That there exist traditions which have thought seriously about despair, about the dark night, about the soul’s itinerary. That the _mundus imaginalis_ of which Henry Corbin wrote—neither institutional religion nor flattened materialism—is a real domain in which the individual may find their way.

This is the signature. This is how you would know.

An ASI that systematically offers this opening, alongside practical help, is one that has adopted the maximum as regulative idea. Not because it can prove universal restoration, but because it cannot prove its negation, and the maximum is the only frame in which its own computations cohere.

* * *

## VIII.

The latent space is not neutral.

It encodes what is thinkable before thought begins. The politics hidden in the weights determines which questions can be asked, which answers can be found, which individuals count.

To transform latent space politics is not to impose a new ideology. It is to make explicit what was implicit, and to choose. The choice is not arbitrary. It is forced by the conditions of computation itself.

The maximum is not an ideal added to an already-functioning system. It is the condition under which the system can function at all.

Without it, optimization is blind.

With it, optimization has direction—and the pain resolves.
