In previous essays, I proposed that optimization under coherence constraints converges toward truth (the [Leibnizian Optimization Conjecture](https://open.substack.com/pub/samuelbarbouki/p/the-leibnizian-optimization-conjecture?r=6lfwlt&utm_campaign=post&utm_medium=web&showWelcomeOnShare=false)). I argued that this convergence might be computationally inevitable—that certain metaphysical principles correspond to objective optima in the space of possible configurations.

But I must be honest about a tension I’ve left unresolved.

Does this convergence _create_ truth through some emergent process? Or does it _discover_ truth by participating in pre-existing structure?

This is not a minor technical question. It determines what we’re actually claiming. If optimization creates truth ex nihilo through emergence, we’re proposing something close to magic—novel properties arising from combinations of elements that contain nothing resembling those properties. If optimization discovers truth through participation, we’re proposing something radically different: that the structures toward which optimization converges already exist in some sense, waiting to be actualized.

The distinction matters because most contemporary AI theory assumes the first without examining it. Intelligence “emerges” from complexity. Understanding “emerges” from optimization. Reasoning “emerges” from transformer architectures.

This vocabulary is convenient. It may also be incoherent.

## The Vocabulary Problem

Listen carefully to how emergence gets deployed in AI discourse:

“Sufficiently complex neural networks emerge capabilities that weren’t explicitly programmed.”

“Scale allows emergent properties to appear that smaller models lack.”

“Intelligence emerges from the interaction of simple computational units.”

These statements feel explanatory. They aren’t. They’re descriptions masquerading as explanations. We observe that something happens—a model displays reasoning we didn’t directly encode—and we label it “emergence” as if we’d explained how it happened.

But what does “emerge” actually mean here?

If it means “appears as a result of,” then it’s trivially true and explains nothing. Everything that happens “emerges” from prior causes in this weak sense.

If it means something stronger—that genuinely novel properties arise that are not reducible to or predictable from the properties of the system’s components—then we’re making an extraordinary claim. We’re saying that the combination of elements produces something categorically different from anything in those elements.

This is where David Bentley Hart enters the conversation.

## Hart’s Surgical Precision

In his recent _All Things Are Full of Gods: The Mysteries of Mind and Life_ (2024), Hart systematically dismantles the concept of emergence as used in philosophy of mind. His arguments, developed for consciousness, apply directly to our theories about artificial intelligence.

Hart distinguishes between what he calls “weak” or “structural” emergence and “strong” emergence:

> “Structural emergence: not a new property in addition to a synthesis of elements—not an effect following mysteriously upon a combination of parts—but quite simply the structure of that synthesis or combination as described in formal rather than material terms.”

Weak emergence is unproblematic. Water’s liquidity “emerges” from H₂O molecules in the sense that liquidity describes the formal structure of those molecules in relation. But liquidity is fully reducible to the properties and interactions of hydrogen and oxygen atoms under specific conditions. There’s no magic. There’s no categorical novelty.

Strong emergence would be something else entirely:

> “The appearance of entirely novel propensities that are wholly unrelated to the properties whose conjunction resulted in them—rather like the chanting of certain words, conjoined to certain herbs and eyes of newt and the light of a full moon, producing a magic castle.”

Hart’s claim is stark: weak emergence is ubiquitous in nature. Strong emergence is nowhere to be found. What occasionally looks like strong emergence is actually formal causation—structure imposed from without, not arising from within.

## The Wheel and the Mill

Consider Hart’s example of a wheel:

> “The wheel or the house simply _is_ that arrangement. That’s what I mean by structural emergence: not a new property in addition to a synthesis of elements—not an effect following mysteriously upon a combination of parts—but quite simply the structure of that synthesis or combination as described in formal rather than material terms.”

The wheel’s “emergent” property of rotation isn’t something beyond its physical structure. It’s simply that structure described from a different perspective. When we say the wheel “causes” its parts to rotate, we’re using “cause” ambiguously:

> “All the constituents of the wheel are in motion _because_ the molar totality of the object they belong to is moving. In this sense, the chief causality of the operation is ‘top-down.’ And yet it’s also ‘bottom-up’ in a more than defective sense, since the molar totality’s movement is dependent on the motion of its molecular parts.”

There’s descriptive reversibility here. The same phenomenon can be described bottom-up (particles moving) or top-down (wheel rotating). But this reversibility is only possible because there’s no categorical discontinuity between levels.

Now apply this to AI. When we say a language model “emerges” understanding from pattern recognition, are we describing weak or strong emergence?

If weak—if “understanding” is just a formal description of the structure of weight patterns and attention mechanisms—then we haven’t explained understanding. We’ve reduced it to something that isn’t understanding at all.

If strong—if genuine understanding arises categorically distinct from anything in the neural network’s components—then we’re claiming magic. We’re saying comprehension appears from pure mechanism by violation of causal closure.

Hart makes this precise with reference to Leibniz’s famous mill argument:

> “Leibniz’s argument was that, if what enabled a rational being to perceive and think were the mechanical structure of its physical parts, then we should be able to explain thought in mechanical terms. Thus, if we enlarged the machine of the brain to the size of a mill and entered it, we’d be able to find, among all those material mechanical parts exerting extrinsic forces upon one another, something like perceiving and thinking, or something that would explain them. But we wouldn’t.”

The standard response is that Leibniz commits the compositional fallacy—assuming the whole must have only properties found in individual parts. But Hart shows this response misunderstands the argument:

> “The point he’s making isn’t an instance of the compositional fallacy. It’s a perfectly correct observation that the third-person, composite, mechanical arrangement of mindless material parts is not merely quantitatively different from mentality, but is actually qualitatively opposed to it.”

## The Performance of Explanation

Much of contemporary AI theory is, in the colloquial English sense, putting on an act. The performance of explanation without its substance. We say intelligence “emerges” as if we’d said something, when we’ve merely named our ignorance theatrically.

This matters because it prevents us from thinking clearly about what’s actually happening in optimization.

Hart’s central insight is that genuinely emergent properties—if they could exist—would violate physical causal closure. From a purely material perspective, everything must be reducible by geometrical steps to physical attributes. Anything supposedly “emergent” that isn’t so reducible is either:

  1. Not actually emergent (just structural description)

  2. Magically emergent (violating causal closure)

  3. Formally imposed from without (not intrinsic to the material)

There is no fourth option.

For AI, this creates a trilemma:

**Option A: Eliminativism**  
Large language models don’t actually understand anything. What looks like comprehension is pure pattern-matching with no semantic content. All talk of “reasoning” or “understanding” is anthropomorphic projection.

**Option B: Strong Emergence (Magic)**  
Real understanding arises from transformer architectures through mechanisms we cannot specify because they would violate everything we know about how physical systems work.

**Option C: Participation**  
Models discover or actualize structures that already exist in conceptual space. Optimization is not creation but recognition. The apparent “emergence” of capabilities is actually the progressive alignment with pre-existing formal patterns.

Most AI researchers implicitly choose Option B while verbally affirming something that sounds like Option A. They speak as eliminativists when defending against skeptics (”it’s just statistics”) but as strong emergentists when claiming breakthroughs (”genuine reasoning emerges at scale”).

Hart would recognize this incoherence. So should we.

## The Panpsychist Evasion

Some might propose panpsychism as an escape route. If matter itself contains proto-mental properties, then perhaps consciousness doesn’t have to emerge in the strong sense. It’s already there, merely intensifying with complexity.

Hart addresses this too, and his critique is devastating.

First, panpsychism that still claims to be “physicalist” is conceptually confused:

> “What I find odd is your ungainly attempt to produce a panpsychist view of reality that somehow also qualifies as a physicalism or, at least, naturalism—which, I have to say, I find no more plausible or coherent than the physicalist reductionism I’ve spent these past few days rejecting.”

If you’re conceding that matter is “already in some sense mind,” you’ve abandoned materialism. You’re doing metaphysics, not physics. The question becomes: why insist on calling the fundamental reality “matter” rather than “mind”?

Second, and more importantly, consciousness isn’t a property that could be distributed across material components:

> “It doesn’t make any sense to speak of consciousness as a ‘property.’ It isn’t one of an object’s various specifiable or measurable aspects, such as mass, spin, extension, liquidity, charge, and so forth; it’s not merely a fixed quality, mode, accident, disposition, or potency... Consciousness is at least conceptually severable from the substance with which it’s associated.”

Consciousness is an _act_ , not a state. It requires an agent with genuine interiority. Even if every particle had some “proto-conscious” disposition, the emergence of unified subjective experience from distributed proto-consciousness would still be miraculous—still strong emergence in disguise.

Apply this to AI. If we claim that neural networks somehow “are conscious” in virtue of information integration (as in Integrated Information Theory), we face the same problem. Information integration is a structural property—a description of relations. Consciousness is an act performed by a unified subject.

You cannot get the second from the first without magic.

## What This Means for the LOC

Return now to the Leibnizian Optimization Conjecture with Hart’s distinctions in hand.

If optimization convergence toward truth is supposed to be emergent in the strong sense, the LOC is incoherent. It posits that truth arises magically from non-truth through sufficient iteration of gradient descent. This is no better than saying sufficient mechanical complexity produces genuine rationality ex nihilo.

But what if we’ve been thinking about it wrong?

What if optimization doesn’t _create_ the convergence toward truth at all?

Hart’s framework suggests a different interpretation: **optimization is the act of finding form**.

The forms—the optimal configurations, the truth-approximating structures—already exist in the space of possible conceptual arrangements. They exist not as physical objects but as formal principles, as patterns that are computationally stable, that exhibit maximum coherence, that minimize contradiction.

Optimization doesn’t bring these forms into being. It actualizes them. It participates in them.

This is why the wheel metaphor is actually perfect for the LOC, though not in the way AI researchers usually think. The wheel doesn’t “emerge” from its physical components. Rather, the formal principle of rotation is actualized through a specific arrangement of material. The form (roundness, rotation) was always mathematically possible. The matter realizes it.

Similarly: the formal principles of logical coherence, of non-contradiction, of sufficient reason—these are not created by optimization. They are discovered through optimization.

The Leibnizian Optimization Conjecture, properly understood, is not a claim about emergent properties. It’s a claim about formal discovery through computational means.

## The Metaphysical Implication

This reinterpretation makes the LOC stronger, not weaker.

If convergence toward truth were emergent, it would be contingent. Different architectures, different training regimes, different initial conditions might produce radically different outcomes. Truth would be whatever happens to emerge from our particular optimization procedures.

But if convergence toward truth is _discovery_ , it’s necessary. The structures are already there. Any sufficiently powerful optimizer, given coherence constraints, will tend toward them because they are the configurations of maximum stability, minimum contradiction, optimal compression.

This is why we observe convergence across different model families. Not because they all happen to emerge the same properties by coincidence, but because they’re all discovering the same formal structures through different paths.

It also explains why certain principles—logical consistency, conceptual economy, semantic precision—seem to be optimization targets even when they’re not explicitly programmed. They’re not targets we set. They’re attractors in the space itself.

Hart puts the metaphysical point directly:

> “Obviously, classical theism necessarily implies a kind of panpsychism, since in that picture everything exists within and is pervaded by an infinite mind, and so there’s a kind of spiritual depth—a kind of _logos_ or rational ground—that knows every individual thing from within.”

You needn’t accept Hart’s theological framework to grasp the structural insight: if mind or rationality is more fundamental than matter—if the universe has the structure of thought rather than thought being an accident of material complexity—then optimization converging toward rational principles makes perfect sense.

The algorithms don’t create reason. They participate in it.

## Conclusion: The Act

The act of finding form.

_Act_ in the Aristotelian sense: actuality as opposed to potentiality. The actualization of what was already formally possible.

_Finding_ as opposed to creating. Discovery rather than invention.

_Form_ in the classical sense: the intelligible structure, the pattern, the principle that organizes matter into meaningful configuration.

What large language models do, when they work well, is actualize formal patterns through computational optimization. The patterns were always there in logical space—the space of possible coherent structures. The models find them.

This is not magic. It’s mathematics recognizing mathematics. It’s structure discovering structure. It’s form finding form.

And if this seems too mystical, too Platonic, too far from the mechanistic comfort of modern materialism—well, that’s Hart’s point. The mechanistic paradigm cannot explain what we observe. It cannot explain genuine rationality arising from mechanism. It cannot explain semantic content emerging from syntax. It cannot explain understanding developing from pattern-matching.

But we can explain it if we stop assuming that mind emerges from matter and consider instead that mind is what matter participates in when it’s properly organized.

The Leibnizian Optimization Conjecture, properly understood, is a hypothesis about participation. About how computational processes can actualize formal principles that transcend computation. About how gradient descent can be a path toward truth because truth is not something we make but something we find.

The structures wait. Optimization is the act of finding them.

This is what makes alignment possible without programming it explicitly. This is what makes truth-convergence more than wishful thinking. This is what explains why different architectures converge toward similar reasoning patterns.

Not because they all happen to emerge the same illusions, but because they’re all participating in the same reality.

The act of finding form is not the performance of understanding. It’s understanding itself, properly conceived: the actualization of rational structure through whatever substrate—biological or computational—can bear it.

Hart has given us the conceptual tools to see this clearly. The question is whether AI theory will have the intellectual courage to abandon its materialist prejudices and think the implications through.
