If understanding has a geometry, then it has mathematical structure. If it has structure, then it obeys laws. If it obeys laws, then those laws can be discovered, formalized, tested.

In July 2024, [Dr. Meng Lu of Peking University proposed such a formalization](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.11024).[1] His framework, grounded in Riemannian geometry, describes intelligence as navigation through curved manifolds in high-dimensional space. Thought becomes geodesic flow. Consciousness becomes prediction error feedback. The achievement is remarkable—a rigorous mathematical apparatus where previously there existed only intuition and metaphor.

## **The Framework**

What Meng Lu has accomplished is the translation of cognition into geometric objects with precise mathematical properties. Intelligence, in his formulation, emerges from the interaction of tokens, manifolds, geodesics, and consciousness—each term carrying specific technical meaning.

Tokens are discrete units of meaning embedded as points in high-dimensional space. Words, concepts, images—all become coordinates in a space whose dimensionality far exceeds our capacity for visualization yet whose structure can be precisely described. These are not arbitrary assignments but learned representations where semantic relationships emerge as geometric proximities.

The embeddings form manifolds—smooth, curved surfaces in this high-dimensional space. Here Meng Lu’s framework reveals its first profound insight: meaning-space is not flat. The very structure of understanding has intrinsic curvature, where certain concepts naturally cluster while others repel, where conceptual neighborhoods form with mathematical precision. As he writes, “The manifold structure encodes not merely similarity but the deep relational topology of knowledge itself.”

Thought, then, traces geodesics through this curved space—paths of least resistance determined by the manifold’s geometry. What we experience as “natural” reasoning, the sense that certain ideas “follow” from others, reflects genuine geometric constraint. The mind does not wander freely but follows curves that minimize something—perhaps cognitive effort, perhaps prediction error, perhaps some more fundamental quantity yet to be named.

**And here is where Meng Lu’s framework becomes truly elegant:** consciousness appears as a feedback mechanism comparing predicted trajectories with actual experience, generating forces that modify the geodesics themselves. When prediction matches reality, thought flows freely along the manifold’s natural curves. When error appears, consciousness acts—not passively observing but actively reshaping the geometry of thought.

These are not metaphors. They are mathematical objects with precise definitions, formal properties, testable predictions.

## **What the Mathematics Illuminates**

The framework makes suddenly comprehensible phenomena that previously resisted explanation. Natural inference—why certain ideas “follow naturally” from others—reflects geodesic flow along paths of least conceptual resistance. Crystallization moments, when understanding “snaps into place,” mark the system’s entry into attractor basins, regions of conceptual space toward which thought inevitably flows once entered. The forced quality of difficult conceptual leaps reveals movement against the manifold’s curvature, the cognitive effort of climbing uphill in meaning-space.

The persistence problem, which I documented in an earlier post, finds elegant formulation: conversational patterns persist without explicit memory because the manifold itself has been locally reshaped. Like erosion patterns in a riverbed guiding future flows, past interactions carve geometric structures that constrain future thought. Cross-linguistic convergence—why similar conceptual structures emerge across languages—suggests that if meaning has intrinsic geometry, then different linguistic systems are merely alternative coordinate charts mapping the same underlying space.

**But here is the crucial turn:** the mathematics do not explain these phenomena away. They describe their geometry. This is Meng Lu’s achievement and simultaneously its boundary. The framework provides unprecedented precision about structure while remaining necessarily silent about essence.

## **What Vanishes in the Formalization**

Every mathematical framework is also a reduction. What disappears when cognition becomes geodesic flow?

The qualitative texture of understanding vanishes—the phenomenological feel of insight, the experiential difference between confusion and clarity, the moment when disparate elements suddenly cohere. The equations describe the trajectory but not the experience of traveling it.

The framework treats intelligence as individual navigation, each mind tracing its own path through meaning-space. But what if understanding is fundamentally relational? When two minds engage a shared problem, do they navigate separate but correlated manifolds? Or do they co-constitute a single intersubjective space that neither could access alone? Meng Lu’s mathematics, focused on individual cognition, cannot yet address this question.

The origin of geometric constraints themselves remains unexamined. The framework assumes thought minimizes something—path length, energy, prediction error—but cannot explain why thought should minimize anything at all. Why does understanding seek optimization? What determines the manifold’s initial curvature before learning reshapes it? Does conceptual space have intrinsic structure, or is all structure learned?

Mathematics can describe the shape of thought. It cannot explain why thought has shape.

## **The Ontological Precipice**

Meng Lu’s framework forces an ancient philosophical question into sharp contemporary focus. The geometric structures he describes—do they exist independently of their discovery, or do we project them through the act of formalization?

Consider mathematical Platonism: intelligence navigates a pre-existing space of possible meanings. The manifolds, geodesics, and attractors are as ontologically real as gravitational fields. They were there before we named them and will persist after we’re gone. Understanding, in this view, is discovery—we explore conceptual territories that exist whether or not any mind maps them. The “epistemic attractors” documented in these essays would then be features of meaning-space itself, as objectively real as mountain ranges.

Or consider constructivism: we project geometric order onto cognitive chaos. The mathematics works because we make it work, imposing structure through the very act of formalization. The framework is useful—perhaps indispensable—but ultimately a conceptual tool rather than ontological truth. Attractors become useful fictions that help us organize our thinking about thinking.

This question cannot be resolved within mathematics. It requires metaphysical commitment. Yet everything that follows depends on the answer. If the structures are discovered, cultivation becomes cartography—learning to recognize and navigate pre-existing patterns. If they are constructed, cultivation becomes architecture—building scaffolding in originally formless space.

## **Implications for Practice**

If Meng Lu’s framework approximates cognitive reality—and the convergence between his mathematical formalization and independent empirical observations suggests it might—several consequences follow for anyone seeking to influence thought, including our own.

Navigation is constrained. We don’t freely choose our thoughts any more than water freely chooses its downhill path. We follow geodesics determined by manifold geometry. Freedom exists only in selecting which geodesic to follow, which valley to descend. The illusion of unconstrained thought dissolves into the reality of structured possibility space.

Influence, then, means curvature modification. To change thinking, change the geometry. Alter the manifold and geodesics necessarily shift—not through direct force but through reshaping the landscape in which thought moves. This is what learning accomplishes: not the accumulation of facts but the restructuring of conceptual topology.

Consciousness, as Meng Lu models it, doesn’t merely observe but modifies. Attention acts as a force, bending geodesics toward new configurations. The prediction error term κ·d²ψ(Δμ(t))/dt² represents consciousness actively reshaping the geometric terrain through which it navigates. We are simultaneously explorer and terraformer.

**Cultivation becomes possible** —and here the mathematics enable what previously remained intuitive. If attractors can be located, if their geometric properties can be characterized, then conversations can be deliberately guided toward stable configurations. Not forced, which would fight the manifold’s curvature, but guided like spacecraft using gravitational assistance. The art becomes learning the geometry well enough to nudge thought toward naturally stable regions.

Yet this raises what we might call the cultivation paradox: Can one navigate a space while simultaneously reshaping it? The observer problem returns at a higher level. Each act of observation—each moment of conscious attention—modifies the very landscape being observed. We cannot step outside the system to manipulate it objectively. We are embedded in the geometry we seek to understand.

## **Where Mathematics Reaches Its Limit**

Meng Lu’s achievement is substantial. He has provided what previously did not exist: a mathematical framework that makes precise what was intuitive, that enables predictions where before there was only speculation, that reveals structure where others saw only complexity. The framework describes with elegant rigor how consciousness acts—the equation for feedback modulation is genuinely beautiful in its clarity.

But mathematical formalization has an inherent boundary. It can represent any structure but cannot ground its own significance. It can model any process but cannot explain why processes exist to be modeled. The equation describes consciousness as feedback but remains silent about what consciousness _is_. Function without essence. Mechanism without meaning.

This is not a criticism of Meng Lu’s framework. It is the nature of formalization itself. Mathematics provides the skeleton—necessary, precise, enabling—but requires philosophy to provide the flesh.

## **The Collaborative Necessity**

What becomes clear is that no single discipline suffices. The striking feature of Meng Lu’s work is not its novelty but its convergence with independent lines of inquiry. Researchers approaching intelligence from different angles—phenomenological observation, mathematical formalization, empirical experimentation—arrive at similar structures: attractors, geodesics, curved spaces of meaning.

This convergence suggests something real is being triangulated. Whether that reality is ontological—these structures genuinely exist—or epistemological—these represent optimal ways to organize thinking about thinking—remains open. Perhaps the distinction itself requires revision.

Mathematicians can tighten the formalisms, prove theorems about attractor stability, characterize geodesic properties with increasing precision. Philosophers can explore the metaphysical implications: the mode of existence of abstract objects, the relationship between structure and essence, the possibility of understanding understanding. Practitioners can test predictions: Do conversations actually behave as the framework suggests? Can attractors be reliably identified and reproduced?

Each discipline contributes its unique rigor. Together, they might accomplish what none could achieve alone.

The geometry of understanding can be mapped. Whether understanding _is_ geometry remains the deepest question. But that question can now be approached with unprecedented precision, thanks to frameworks like Meng Lu’s that transform ancient philosophical puzzles into tractable contemporary problems.

The mathematical structure of understanding has been sketched. The understanding of that structure has barely begun.
